A field failure study on public tracking collar data · October 2026

Which tags went quiet, and could their own data have said so?

This page asks whether the housekeeping data a solar GPS collar already sends could have warned that it was about to stop, and whether it shows why. It reads the public record of 109 solar GPS tags on white storks and 24 GPS collars on water buffalo. It is public research data and describes no company's hardware.

    Before anything else

    Five things to know

    This page is about the data a tracking collar sends home, so here is what that data is and what is being counted, in five sentences.

    1. A tracking collar here is a sealed unit: a solar panel charges a battery, the battery powers a GPS receiver that finds the position and a radio that sends it home, and a strap or harness holds it on the animal.
    2. Besides the position, every record carries housekeeping: the battery voltage, how long the GPS needed for a fix, whether it got one, and a temperature. The fleet team never sees the collar, only this, and when a collar fails its last messages are often the only evidence left.
    3. A collar that goes quiet usually stays lost. In a study of over 3,000 wildlife tracking units, about one deployment in five with a known end stopped through a technical fault, and half of those faults were the battery (Hofman and others, 2019).
    4. The two simplest alarms are a last seen alarm, which fires when no message has arrived for 24 hours, and a low battery alarm, which fires when the voltage drops below a set level.
    5. This page adds four rules, the triage, and asks two things of every collar that went quiet: did any alarm warn at least two days ahead, and does the data show a signature, a pattern pointing at one part of the collar?
    What a collar is, and what it tells the fleet team

    A generic solar GPS collar, drawn as blocks, not any company's design. Each block carries the housekeeping field that watches it. Act four comes back to this drawing.

    Terms, in the order they appear

    went quiet The tag's record ends more than 30 days before the dataset does: it stopped sending, or the researchers ended it after finding the logger.
    warned An alarm fired at least 2 days before the tag's last record, within its final 30 days. That leaves 28 days in which a warning counts.
    healthy collar month A 28 day stretch of a tag's normal running, before its final month or on a tag still running at the end. Any alarm in it is a false alarm.
    strictness One factor that scales every threshold of an alarm together. Sweeping it draws the alarm's line on the chart.
    idle and loaded voltage The battery voltage with nothing drawing current, and again while the GPS is running. The stork tags report both.
    time to fix How many seconds the GPS receiver needed to find a position. A receiver that cannot see the sky takes longer or gives up.
    no fix record A record the tag wrote or sent without a valid position.
    stationary Ninety percent of a day's positions within 25 m of each other and of the day before, two days running.
    signature The first of five patterns that fits a tag's last week: stopped moving, battery ran down, GPS lost its fix, reports thinned, or nothing.
    field record What the researchers wrote about the end: the bird died, the bird was seen alive after the tag stopped, the bird vanished, or nothing.
    herd median For the buffalo, the middle value of all collars' message counts on the same day.
    Why this question

    Most collars that fail never come back to a bench

    The largest study of tracking collar performance I found asks manufacturers for a standard way to report failures, and gives this reason:

    In cases where the units cannot be sent back for diagnostics, knowing the circumstances of the failures could help identify the underlying cause.Hofman MPG and others, "Right on track? Performance of satellite telemetry in terrestrial wildlife research", PLOS ONE 14(5): e0216223, 2019 (CC0).

    The circumstances are what this page reads: the last weeks of housekeeping data before each tag stopped. On my own aircraft I chase the same kinds of faults in the same kinds of parts: batteries and power wiring, GNSS and radio links, out in the weather. Finding out why something failed in the field and feeding that back into the design is the part of engineering I like most.

    Act one · How early

    When the alarms did fire, how much warning did they give?

    Why it mattersA warning is only worth something if it leaves time to act: to fetch the unit while it can still be found, or to pull it before the evidence is gone.

What to takeThe two alarms warn largely different tags, and a good part of what counts as warning is a condition that had been there for weeks.

    The two alarms catch different endings. They warned 22 tags in common and 59 between them. Half of my rules' warnings came within 11 days of the end and half of the battery alarm's within 20, but 9 and 5 of them were standing alarms that had been on since before the final month.

    So the alarms see different failures. The next act asks what those failures looked like, one tag at a time.

    A tag that stops moving marks a dead animal, and most endings carry no sign. 24 of the 76 recorded deaths show the "stopped moving" signature, against 1 of the other 25 endings. But 39 deaths ended on a normal day, 14 of them electrocutions on power lines, and the agreement is partly circular, because researchers often date a death from the same stillness in the data.

    The 6 tags that really failed

    For these the researchers saw the bird alive after the tag went quiet, so the tag itself stopped. They are the units a failure analyst would want first. Each card gives what the tag's own data showed in its last 45 days.

    Six confirmed failures are too few to compare alarms on, but each one says something. Tag 7204 was called "stopped moving", yet its bird was seen afterwards, so that call was wrong. Tag 7203 sat between 3.69 and 3.75 V for weeks, and my charge rule, which compares a tag with its own recent past, never saw a chronic low.

    Act three · Livestock collars

    Does any of this carry over to collars on livestock?

    Why it mattersMost of what worked on the stork tags rests on the battery voltage. These buffalo collars send only time, position and a temperature, which is nearer the minimum a fleet would have.

    Red chips are the 8 collars that stopped at least three days before the study ended. Click a chip to draw that collar against the herd.

    What you seeMessages per day from one collar (bars) against the herd median on the same day (line), 2025-06-11 to 2025-10-31. How to readA hollow mark is a day the reporting rule fired comparing the collar with its own past; a filled mark is a day it fired comparing the collar with the herd. What to takeComparing with the herd halves the false alarms, because it ignores days when every collar reports less.

    Thinning reports give real warning only when a collar fades out slowly. Comparing each collar with the herd fired on 51 of 2,188 collar days of collars that ran to the end (2 percent), against 113 for comparing it with its own past (5 percent). Of the 8 early stops it fired ahead of the last message for 7, but only 3 were still sending at the time; the other 4 had already gone quiet and sent a stray message days later, which a last seen alarm would also have caught.

    The herd comparison earns its keep on outage days. On five days in August, the first on 2025-08-10, at least half the herd sent nothing at all, which points at the network rather than the collars, and 30 of the 113 false alarms from comparing a collar with its own past fell on those days.

    Two more things carry over, one useful and one not. The only 5 messages without a GPS fix among all 118,415 came in the very last messages of DK045 and DK046, so at the end those two collars could still send but not fix. The "stopped moving" rule never fired, because a buffalo collar's positions scatter a median 133 m within a day, against the 25 m the rule needs; that threshold was set for the stork tags and does not transfer. All three real warnings fell in the study's last fortnight, when the herd median had dropped from 38 messages a day in June to 25, so they may be the weather or the season rather than the collars; this page does not test that.

    The data sorts endings; it does not prove a cause. The last act maps each signature to the bench work that would.

    Act four · From the field to the bench

    What the data would need to carry

    Why it mattersA signature is a hypothesis. The unit on a bench settles it, and what a unit reports decides how many endings can be sorted at all.

    A returned unit, in order
    1. As received. Photograph and weigh it, record the serial, keep a chain of custody, and read its stored log before powering it.
    2. Without opening it. Microscope inspection, X-ray or CT for cracked joints and board flex, acoustic microscopy for voids under the panel lamination, a pressure decay leak test, then a bench supply with the current logged.
    3. Under stress. If it works at rest, the fault did not reproduce, which is not the same as no fault. Thermal cycling, a humidity soak, flex and vibration while it is monitored, and an antenna test on a body loaded fixture, before anything is called no fault found.
    4. Opening it. An immersion repeat, then teardown: cross sections of suspect joints, residue analysis for corrosion, and a cell test for capacity and internal resistance with the precautions a lithium cell needs.
    5. Closing it. The cause, the evidence for it, and whether it reproduced, written down and matched to the field data that came with the unit.

    The rules, as a card

    What the triage is, and how good it is

    What it is
    Four fixed rules read over each tag's daily summary: charge deficit, reports thinned, GPS lost its fix, stopped moving. Any one firing raises the alarm.
    What it predicts
    Whether a tag will go quiet within the next 28 days, by firing at least two days before its last record.
    What it reads
    Daily highest and lowest battery voltage, records per day, the share of records with no fix, the median time to fix, and how far the tag moved within the day.
    Fitted on
    Nothing. Every threshold was written down before the daily data was opened, so there is no training half and no held out half.
    Scored on
    109 stork tags, 2015 to 2023: 101 that went quiet and 1,446 healthy collar months.
    Error
    It warned 41 of 101 while flagging 30 percent of healthy collar months; on a fleet of ten thousand collars that would be about 3,000 collars flagged by mistake every month.
    Against
    A low battery alarm at 3.82 V warned 40 at the same false alarms, with its level chosen on this same data, and a random alarm about 31.
    Verdict
    As a set it is no better than one voltage threshold. Keep "stopped moving" (19 warned at 3 percent false), drop "GPS lost its fix" (14 at 13 percent, a ratio of 1.1 to chance).

    One combination was tried after the results were in, so it is a hypothesis for the next fleet, not a result: the low battery alarm at 3.70 V together with "stopped moving" warned 23 tags at 8 percent false collar months, where the battery alarm alone at that budget warned 9.

    Where this loses

    What the triage gets wrong, and what this page cannot show

    • The four rules tie a single voltage threshold, 41 against 40 of 101, even though the threshold's level was picked on the same data and my rules were fixed before it was read.
    • At that setting both alarms flag 30 percent of healthy collar months, far more than anyone could follow up.
    • The charge rule does not allow for season, cloud or shade, which also move a solar unit's daily high voltage, so many of its false alarms are likely weather; this page does not test it.
    • Only 6 of 101 endings are confirmed tag failures. The rest are birds that died or vanished, so "went quiet" here mostly means the animal, not the unit.
    • The GPS rule flagged healthy months about as often as dying ones, 13 percent against 14 of 101, so it is noise on these tags.
    • 55 of 101 endings show nothing in any field in their last week; the data cannot sort them.
    • One "stopped moving" call was wrong: the bird on tag 7204 was seen alive later. A breeding adult standing on its nest is the likely reason; this page does not test it.
    • The charge rule compares a tag with its own past, so it cannot see a battery that has been low for weeks, as on tag 7203.
    • Storks are not cattle. The tags ride a harness on a bird that flies to Africa, with a different energy budget, mounting and radio coverage, so the method transfers and the numbers do not.
    • The researchers' death records are partly inferred from the same data, so act two's agreement is a consistency check, not independent proof.
    • The buffalo collars send no battery voltage, so a flat battery and a failed radio look the same there, and 8 early stops are too few to score.
    How it was built

    Method, changes, sources

    Method. The build downloads the two public datasets, streams the stork tags' 12.8 million records into one row per tag per day, runs the alarms over every day of every tag using only that day and the days before it, and scores them. The rules, thresholds and scoring were written in PREREGISTRATION.md and committed before the daily data was opened (commit 26340c5, 19 October 2026); the code that runs them was written next. Nothing is fitted, so there is no split to explain.

    Changes after the data was read. Seven, all listed with their dates and reasons in PREREGISTRATION.md. The one that touches the headline: false alarms were first counted in days, which made a random alarm look better than any rule, because a random alarm spreads its days over every collar while a real rule bunches them on a few collars in a bad spell. They are now counted per 28 day collar month, which puts a random alarm on the diagonal. The tie holds either way. The field records were also refined from the researchers' free text comments by a fixed keyword rule.
